A lazy afternoon
What a splendid afternoon! It was as if Spring had gotten impatient of waiting for Autumn and Winter to have their turn and pushed on ahead. Sun-soaked, fragrant with wildflowers, with the occasional reet awakening from its half-doze to chirrup a few languid notes, before settling back into silence. Bumblebees, heavy with nectar, floated lazily through patches of clover, dropping down occasionally to gather a bit more, before spreading their wings so the soft breeze could carry them home with their sweet treasure. It was precisely the sort of day that was perfect for whatever you wished, whether it was sailing on a small dinghy up the Stret River, or snoozing in a hammock, with your book lying forgotten across your belly...

The Lioness crept quietly through a more densely forested area at the edge of the meadow, more silent than her namesake, stalking her prey. She lifted her field glasses to her face, and carefully scanned the underbrush. A slight movement drew her attention, over there, just beneath those brambles. She knelt down slowly, and drew back a curtain of thorny growth with a gloved hand.

A small animal darted out, squeaking anxiously at her, before fleeing. The Lioness didn't even give her another look, her attention on what was beneath all those blackberries.

Ahh, there they were, half a dozen baby leets. So tiny! So young, their eyes were still closed, although all their fur had grown in. She picked up one, cradling it in her hands. It nuzzled blindly at her thumb, not at all alarmed by the foreign presence. So small, so vulnerable... , she thought. She gently stroked its back with her fingertip. She slid her finger under its chin, and snapped its little neck.

She repeated the process five more times with the rest of the litter, then sat back on her heels, looking mournfully at the tiny bodies. She didn't cry, although her throat was tight, and there was a stinging behind her eyes.

She reached for her sack, gently placing the little bodies inside. It wasn't an empty sack - it already contained 86 other baby leets - a full, sad day's work. She shook her head. Environmentalists. Stupid, foolish people. About three weeks earlier, some radical group had broken into an Omni-Med research facility, and 'liberated' several leets. Liberated them into the lovely meadows just outside of Rome, where the three lab specimens had ample time to breed with the local population.

The leets in question had been part of an ongoing experiment in new genetic therapy drugs - the goal was a vaccination, of sorts, that would help young children survive the death/resurrecting process. Those leets had been injected with an advanced type of growth cell... and since the experiment was still in its early stages... the results hadn't been... quite normal....

What 'quite normal' meant, she wasn't precisely sure. She hadn't had access to the data gathered on the mutated offspring of those lab leets, but a trusted colleague and erstwhile mentor had whispered to her that this could wipe out the entire healthy leet population. To say nothing of what the secondary effects might be on the environment. And while Omni-Med had discreetly kept it out of the media, the silent alarm had gone out. She had received her orders, along with a dozen other naturalists: all baby leets in a 100-kilometer radius were to be rounded up and put down. All adult leets were to be injected with a fertility drug.

There was a flapping sound overhead, and some scolding, as one reet chased another away from its tree branch. She didn't even notice, as she sat there, smoldering with anger and resentment. Those stupid protesters - in proving their point, and freeing a couple of otherwise-well-cared-for leets, they had put the entire leet population of Rubi-Ka at risk. And dammit, she wished Omni-Med would clean up their own damned mess. Just once, she'd love to see one of those snooty lab docs come out here with a sack, tramp through the underbrush, and kill one baby animal after another.

The Lioness had sat still for so long, that the mother leet, no longer afraid, came running back to her nest, squeaking anxiously as she searched for her babies. The Lioness exhaled softly, her hand gently easing a tiny dart-gun out of her boot. With one quick movement, she lined up the shot, and fired, the tiny needle hitting the animal in the flank, and deploying the drug almost instantly. The leet sank to the ground, fast asleep. The drug was a time-released hormone, designed to keep the animal infertile for the next six weeks. Then, over a period of about six more weeks, the leet's reproductive system would gradually return to normal. By then, she fervently hoped, they would be able to say, with authority, that there were no mutant strains of leet out in the wilds.

Tucked away in a basement right next to 'Neuters 'r' Us' in Newland City, there is a small, private establishment called the 'The Eight Ball Social Club'. The club itself consists of row upon row of pool tables and a functional but cheerless bar area. It is a venue petty crooks and hardened gamblers frequent, not the sort of place you'd take your family.

The 'Eight Ball' also happens to be the place where Rorybonez does his business these days. Rory is the front-man of the World Leet Fighting Federation, an organisation that runs clandestine leet fights. As such he fits the petty crook and hardened gambler profile rather well.

Rory sits at one of the tables by the bar. Next to him is Strimps, a simple-minded thug who also works for the WLFF, and Dr Symms, a nervous looking nano in a conservative business suit. Dr Symms is a middle ranking, disaffected Omni-Med employee. He has a small cage resting on his lap, the content of which is the prime reason for this meeting.

"So, doc, let's see this mutant leet, shall we?" suggested Rory?

"Here?" Dr Symms replies, aghast.

"Sure, here. Don't worry the cops don't come here except on pay day and the regulars know how to mind their own business." Rory takes the cage away from Dr Symms. While with one hand he opens the cage, with the other he reaches inside and grabs the animal. Rory then brings the leet close to him to examine it.

"Careful boss. The doc said its a mutie. What if it shoots deathrays from it's eyes?"

"Shut up, Strimps." says Rory as he smacks the back of his bodyguard's head with his free hand.

"Ouch."

After a cursory examination, Rory lowers the leet so that it rests on his laps. "Well doc, just looks like a regular leet to me."

"Look at his teeth." suggests Symms.

Rory pries the leets mouth open. "Hmmm, teeth are good shape." He releases the beasts mouth. "Okay, so ya taught a leet to floss. Personally I'm impressed, but this is not really what the WLFF is looking for."

"No, no no." protests Dr Symms. "The teeth are in perfect shape because this specimen is only one week old."

"One week? What the hell you been feeding it?"

"That is not your concern. The point is this new species of mutant leet will be stronger, faster and smarter than any existing leet. If this is not worth your credits, then we have nothing more to say to each other."

"Whoa, cool it doc. I need to think about this a sec." Rory takes a sip of his beer carefully while studying the leet.

Feeling itself the centre of attention, the leet says "R u nubi?"

"You absolutely sure this is not just a regular leet?"

Dr Symms nods emphatically.

"Okay then, I'll buy it off ya. But if this fuzzball does not turn out to be a champion of the likes of Leetal Weapon or Dr Uber, you are gonna be in a whole sea of trouble, doc."

"I am sure you won't be disappointed." replies Symms.

*two weeks later...*

Her hands were full, so she had to use her elbow to mash a button indicating a visitor to the Omni-Med Zoology Division Offices. She smiled, noting the two cameras that swung around and zoomed in on her. She wondered idly what the horrified receptionist would say, at the sight of her mud-caked boots and filthy leather clothing. The thought elicited a small grin, which was interrupted by a buzzer. With a whoosh! the metal door smoothly slid open.

She strode in, a small square cage in each hand, a large leather bag slung over each shoulder, along with the standard executioners dangling from each hip. She saw the slight figure behind the receptionist's desk and her eyes widened, in delighted surprise.

"Cookie!", she said, smiling at the small, snowy-haired woman. "I didn't think to see you here!" She had to squash the impulse to drop everything on the floor, and hug her tightly.

"Well, Dr. Stewart needed my assistance here at the lab, and the receptionist is on her lunch break, I offered to cover for her", said Cookie, her bright blue eyes twinkling. Nobody was sure how old Cookie was, not even the woman herself, but there was no sign of age or infirmity as she hopped back around the desk and pushed a button.

"Dr. Stewart", she said cheerily, "Miss DeWinter is here to see you". She made a little face at the girl as she said this. Miss DeWinter? Dr. Stewart had known her since she was a baby. He had, in fact, delivered her, back when he was still practicing medicine... on humans...

"Excellent", boomed a voice, "send her in". Lionelle raised her eyebrows at Cookie, who mouthed "Omni-bigwigs in there with him". Lionelle gave her a quick wink, and walked into Dr. Stewart's office-laboratory.

There he was, dear man, tall and thin, slightly stooped shoulders. Wiry white hair standing up on the top of his head. Charles Patrick Stewart, MD, PhD, DVM. And the man who had been more her father than Lionel ever had.

She squashed the disloyal thought. The Great Lionel DeWinter, well, he couldn't be held to the standards of mere mortal men. She gave her head a quick shake, and watched affectionately as Dr. Stewart continued his earnest conversation with two men in Omni-Tek Executive suits. Bah, executives. They looked past him, at her, their faces scornful at her muddy, disheveled appearance. When they spent three straight weeks combing the wilds, just to cover for a high-level Omni-Med exec who had let some pretty face sweet-talk him into codes for the lab, well, then they could sneer all they liked. She bit back an impulse to stick out her tongue at them, and cleared her throat instead. At the sound, Dr. Stewart turned around, smiling apologetically.

"Lionelle", he smiled, "so good to see you." His eyes dropped to the cages in her hands. "And what have we here? Our little rogues, I think". He smiled at the sight of the two leets she had recovered from the wilderness, small red Omni-Tek tags attached to their right ears. He took them from her, set the cages on the counter, examining them carefully.

She took one leather bag off her shoulder, holding it out. "These are the specimens I was collecting." She gave a delicate cough as one of the execs peered at it curiously. "Sir, I wouldn't - ", she started, as the man peered into the bag, half-full of dead baby leets. He quickly pushed it back at her, his face a lighter shade of pale green. He nodded at Dr. Stewart. "Doctor", he said, by way of farewell. Without another word, he and his colleague left the lab. It was all Lionelle could do not to burst into laughter.

What a day, one stifled impulse after another.

Dr. Stewart was still busy examining the animals. She smiled. He had such a gentle way with animals. He was a quiet man, reserved - some would say shy. To look at him you would never realize the brilliance of the man - a medical doctor, with a PhD in notum biochemistry, and a degree in veterinary medicine to boot. He had left Omni-Med decades ago, to take charge of the new Rome Municipal Zoo. But Omni-Med still valued his brilliance, and kept him on the payroll as an independent researcher. Thus he had a state-of-the-art laboratory, as well as staff, here at OM.

But the Zoo. That was his great love. It had been his vision, from start to finish. A safe place for interested observers to see the variety of flora and fauna on Rubi-Ka. A safe haven for wild creatures who had been injured or ill, nursed back to health, yet unable to survive in the wilderness. And a retirement home for the research animals used by Omni-Med. Omni-Tek didn't officially own the zoo, it was merely a contributor, the largest public one. The largest overall contributor was actually a private source - the South Rubi-Ka Society. Conquistador Coffee was also a hefty contributor. All three entities made up most of the board of trustees for the zoo. They managed the Zoo Fund, which kept it solvent year after year.

But Dr. Stewart meant so much to Lionelle on a personal basis. He was practically her father. As a matter of fact, he had almost been her father. Her mother was engaged to marry him, when they had gone to see The Lion performing in the Omni-Ent Arena. Celia had taken one look at The Great Lionel DeWinter, and promptly forgot the shy, brilliant man at her side. Lionelle wasn't supposed to know the story, but she'd wormed it out of Nanny Rose once, when the woman had had a bit too much to drink one New Years'....

Dr. Stewart finally remembered the girl standing there, and looked up, smiling. "You've done a fine job. And we've recovered all three animals. So hopefully...", his voice trailed off. She nodded, understanding. There had been a beautiful breed of small songbird, five years back, that had been eradicated when a lab specimen of a similar breed had escaped, carrying a unique biotoxin that had annihilated the species.

Aha! She smiled at him, thinking to distract him. "I did come across this, just before I left Galway County..." she reached inside her vest, and carefully pulled out a baby reet. It sat on the metal table, blinking up at them. It was covered with a combination of soft down, and prickly-looking quills, which would eventually unfurl into feathers. The little creature rocked unsteadily on huge baby feet, and opened its beak, making a series of beseeching baby-bird sounds. It proved the distraction she had hoped for. He flashed her a wide grin, and quickly washed his hands. He gently rubbed the top of its bristly head. "Now this is special", he said. "This is one of the few species we haven't been able to successfully breed in captivity". She nodded, already aware of this. He examined it for a moment, then glanced up, eyes curious. "You found this in the wild, you say?" She nodded. "How very extraordinary", he marvelled. "They usually don't breed at this time of year".

"I found the mother close by to the nest", she said regretfully. "A cat got her. But she managed to do some damage to the cat. She'd put a nice hole in its belly, and its guts were starting to spill out. I had to put the cat out of its misery".

"Good", he replied coolly. He didn't like cats, and she couldn't blame him there. Domestic felines were neither native to Rubi-Ka, nor had they been part of the original terra-forming plan. Someone, homesick for Earth, or perhaps tired of rollerrats, had smuggled one onto the planet. From that first cat had come a fearsome population. Some did hunt roller-rats, while others hunted all the pretty birds and myriad small creatures that made Rubi-Ka unique.

He was saying something, and she smiled, instantly attentive. "I'm giving this baby back to you. I don't have time to care for it properly". She started to protest, but he cut her off with smile, saying, "Stop by the zoo, get some of those monkey-chow biscuits. Soak them in water, and puree them together with peanut butter, some of that jarred baby food - the fruits and vegetables, and some sunflower seeds. You'll want to feed it every couple of hours. Oh, and keep it warm, use a reptile lamp if you need to. Not less than 82 degrees, no higher than 88." She smiled happily, carefully picking up the baby bird. It protested loudly at not being fed yet. How did he know? She'd always wanted one of these... "I'll take very good care of it", she promised. He smiled back. She tiptoed, kissed him on the cheek, and left.

By eight pm, she was ready to crash. The baby bird, fed now for the third time, was dozing in a glass aquarium, with a heat lamp turned on. She was a mess, still wearing her grubby leather clothing, now crusted with dried baby-bird-food. She peeled off the armor, took the hottest shower she could stand, and collapsed into bed.
