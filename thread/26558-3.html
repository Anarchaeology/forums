Windows (In-)Security, the Elephant in the Living Room
There's an old saying about an elephant in the living room; even if nobody wants to talk about it, everybody knows it's there.

For what seem to me to be perfectly honest, fair, and inescapable reasons, Funcom is saying that if your account gets hacked, and it's because your Windows PC isn't secure enough to be on the internet, there isn't anything they can do for you. If you leave your machine just as insecure after the first time it gets hacked, then the second time, they're going to throw your hindquarters out rather than have to keep dealing with hackers taking advantage of the fact that you've knowingly left your machine vulnerable.

And this all makes perfect sense, so long as nobody talks about something, even though everybody on the planet knows it: it takes serious effort by a degreed and certified computer security specialist to reliably secure even one Windows PC. Windows ships right out of the box in a very insecure form, with just about every "port" open and unsecured, because Bill Gates believes that that's how things ought to be. (He has altruistic reasons for this. He's not evil, he's just stupid.)

I never bothered to install a firewall on my machine, despite the fact that it's on a cable modem. Why not, after all the news insisting that cable modem users need firewalls? Because I was a computer security specialist, until recently. 14 years' experience in the field. A little rusty after a couple years out of it, but I'm still pretty familiar with what it takes to hack a machine. And I wasn't worried. I did believe that I knew enough about security vulnerabilities that if I shut down every hackable service on my PC, and kept my Windows version patched, I was immune to hacks, or near enough. Near enough that adding a firewall program, which is just as likely as Windows to have its own buffer-overflow vulnerabilities, wasn't going to make it any safer, just slow my already underpowered machine down further.

So then, in the previous thread where this brouhaha was hashed out the first time, somebody posted a link to a web site that would, with your permission, scan your PC for vulnerabilities. I ran it on mine, just to check my work. It found two. One of them I knew to be hype, an attempt to sell me a firewall I didn't need. The other one disturbed me ... a Windows service I'd barely heard of, didn't turn on, and can not find any way within Windows to turn it off or remove. I found a free utility on the same web site that was written to shut down that service, and used it. Problem solved.

But that's my point! I have a BA in Math/CS, 14 years experience in computer and network security, and I couldn't plug all the vulnerabilities on my own machine! And you have no call to accuse me of being lame for this. Survey after survey shows that most machines on the internet are insecure, especially Windows boxes. But even acres and acres of Linux and Solaris servers are insecure, despite full-time security staffs, because they fail to find out about vulnerabilities and patch them in a timely manner. If it were not so, you would have stopped hearing about distributed denial of service attacks by now.

So yes, I don't see any alternative to Funcom's policy. But some compassion is certainly in order. Because proving you have the competence to download a 7 day free trial or install a boxed CD-ROM does not demonstrate that you have the competence to secure your PC. If Funcom intends to offer this service only to people who are competent to close all the vulnerabilities in Windows, even after a hack ... well, I don't think they'll be bringing in much revenue, let me say that much.

Could It Happen to You? D'uh. Yes.
quote:
Originally posted by Flapsie
so what is the adress to that page where u could check ur pc ???
Here's the web page I used: http://www.grc.com.

And it's not a perfect solution, because you about have to be a current or former security professional to get any use out of it. It gave me one warning and two alerts. I knew the warning was no big deal. I had a considered professional opinion that one of the alerts was BS. Despite all my experience, I had no idea if the second alert was high-vulnerability or if they were just trying to sell me their product, but since it was on a Microsoft service I don't use, I just found a utility to plug that one hole.

Without my experience in the business, if you run that test, you're going to find vulnerabilities ... and not know which ones are important. So you'll end up buying a firewall, whether you needed one or not, and you still won't feel safe, because you'll wonder (eventually) if your firewall software is any more secure than Windows.

And if you're as dumb as the average person or dumber, you'll still end up hacked if somebody thinks your stuff is worth stealing.

They'll figure out your email address, anonymously email you an animated greeting card, you'll open it, and it'll mail them your anarchy.cfg file. Or you'll download some cool new animation or killer utility in a self-extracting archive (either one in .exe form) and it'll do the same thing.

Or they'll send you an email with "a picture of Britney Spears topless" and you won't notice that the file extension isn't .jpg, it's .jpg.scr, and you'll open it anyway, and it'll hack your machine the same way. Outlook Express will warn you that ".scr" files are dangerous, and so will other mail packages. You won't know what ".scr" means, and you'll think it's worth the risk to see Britney Spears topless, so you'll do it anyway. Some of you will. How do I know? Because copies of similar email worms show up in my email inbox every other day. Some of y'all are just that stupid.

Or you'll go to a web page to download "free warez" that somebody sent you the URL to anonymously, and a web script on that page will take advantage of one of several vulnerabilities in Internet Exploder to hack your machine. (Most of those vulnerabilities are patched. But what are the odds that your copy of Internet Explorer is up-to-date on patches? I saw one survey that said only about one in three were.)

Or they'll send you a trojan horse in IRC, or ICQ, or Yahoo Messenger, and you'll run it.

Or you'll use the same account name and password to log into someone's "Anarchy Online Cheats and Exploits" web page, and they'll use that info to hollow out your account.

Or you'll use your dog's name for your password, but put your dog's name on your personal web site, and they'll guess that in a few tries, and clean your account out that way.

And if you do any of these things, or any of the other of dozens of really stupid things that people do on the Internet a million times a day, and none of them will be Funcom's fault.
