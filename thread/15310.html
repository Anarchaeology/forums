over-equipping
I hear ALL kinds of opinions about over-equipping, and would like to see it discussed here, calmly. Personally I feel the rules are fine. If you can figure out a way, within rules (I.E., no hacks or exploits) to equip a more powerful item, using implants, stat-bonus clothes, buffs, etc etc, then great! Its hardly inexpensive to do so, and definitely an effort both in time as well as deed. So let that effort be rewarded. What's the big deal? If you don't want to make the effort, you don't have to, then be happy with what you havebut look, I don't want to digress into a lecture about jealousy. If I could alter the over-equipping rules at all, though, Id make sure its equal opportunity. Some skills and stats can be heavily buffed (eg, brutal thug + major wrangle and even more buffs to raise certain weapon scores sharply) while others can't be buffed at all (eg, INT and PSY armor wearers are screwed compared to STR and STA armor wearers). Id like to see it equal across the board. I know we dont want to see a lvl 10 wearing ql200 items, but if u want a lvl 35 to wear ONLY ql 35 armor or below, why even bother having stats? You really want +ALL+ armor to have as its reqs "must be lvl xxx to equip"? That would be super boring. I actually LIKE all the trouble I hafta go to so I can arm a ql 40 weapon at lvl 30. Its PART OF THE FUN ....dont make it no longer part of the game.

Overequipping in one sense was always in the game, FC intended for you to be able to use implants to boost your abilities and various NFs to temporarily enhance your abilities. The problem is that due to holes in the game system, people were able to stretch Funcom's intention into ways that were not foreseen and did not positively contribute to the game. I've heard certain people get very defensive over this and they tend to blurt out that if OE (as it currently exists) wasn't intended, why are there wrangles and such in the game? That's easy: those are meant to be temporary skill boosts to give you an advantage in a fight or to complete a discrete task. When it expires, you are no longer supposed to benefit from it, so by rights you should not be able to still use the weapon you equipped when wrangled.

quote:
Personally I feel the rules are fine. If you can figure out a way, within rules (I.E., no hacks or exploits) to equip a more powerful item, using implants, stat-bonus clothes, buffs, etc etc, then great! Its hardly inexpensive to do so, and definitely an effort both in time as well as deed. So let that effort be rewarded. What's the big deal? If you don't want to make the effort, you don't have to, then be happy with what you havebut look, I don't want to digress into a lecture about jealousy.
No, this is completely wrong. Let me address the first part: in a game like this, the game rules are framework that lets players arbitrate their actions with respect to each other. Ever play cowboy and indians or army man when you were a kid? Remember getting into arguments over who shot who first? These games are grown-up versions of those games, and the rules are there so you can determine who shot who first without arguments, and so forth. The rules establish the boundaries of the imaginary world that you play in with others.

Problems arise because rule sets are imperfect abstractions and thus can never completely encompass the reality they are trying to model. If players try hard enough, they will always find holes in the rules and either completely circumvent the rules or bend them in ways the designers never intended them to be used. It may bring an advantage to the player who finds the shortcut, but it's at the game's expense, and if the designers reward this behavior, they lose control of their game.

Since rules can never be 100% exploit proof, the thing that must be fought is the player mentality that seeks to undermine the rules. This mentality is called "gamesmanship." Gamesmanship is a term we used in the Army at the combat training centers. Those of you who did training rotations at NTC or CMTC or JRTC know what I am talking about. These were big training facilities that simulated wars to the best level possible with modern technology. Player units and the OPFOR wore sensors and used laser transmitters on their weapons to simulate weapons fire, and you fired a blank from your weapon to activate the laser. However, some players figured out ways to make their transmitters fire without needing to shoot the rifle, enabling them to make "silent kills" (to name just one exploit). Soldiers who do this no longer are playing the simulation and trying to improve their warfighting skills but are turning the game against itself for a personal advantage, which effectively sabotaged the whole effort. This sort of behavior was punished pretty harshly when it was found out. I don't think this behavior is fundamentally different from expoiters or massive OEers in the AO. Of course we aren't sharpening our war skills in AO, but when you turn the game against itself, you undermine the make-believe world you play in and the game degenerates into a competition to see who can find the next loop hole in the rules. It kills the immersion, the sense of being a member of a futuristic society on an alien planet.

Some players need to get a sense of responsibility - you play the game to have fun, but you are also responsible for the collective maintenance of the game world. Think of it like raking up the sand bunker during a round of golf. If you play entirely to indulge yourself at everyone else's cost, you'll contribute to destroying the game world for everyone else, and eventually there will no longer be a game to play. Of course, there will always be players who don't care, so that's why Funcom has to do what they're doing with these patches.

Massive overequipping is gamesmanship because the rules never intended for players to derive permanent benefit from temporary skill buffs. It stands to reason that if you require a certain level of skill to use an item, and you can only have that skill for a few minutes thanks to a buff, you shouldn't be able to use it after the buff wears off. It's no different than the soldiers who hotwired their MILES (laser) transmitters.
