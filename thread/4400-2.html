Maybe if we look at it as a ratio:

average player toughness @ level n
----------------------------------------------------
average mob toughness @ level n

Where toughness is a theoretical factor of average damage over average combat time, average HP, and average AC.

In an ideal world, this should be as close to 1:1 as possible. That is to say, a mob at level n should be yellow to a player at level n - an even fight. This is a primary concern for Funcom; make things too easy, and players will leave for lack of challenge. Make it too hard, and people will split out of frustration.

As average player toughness increases, funcom needs to raise the mob toughness to compensate. Moderate over-equipping (increasing player toughness) requires only a moderate increase of mob toughness by Funcom to compensate.

However, radical over-equipping requires radical mob compensation by Funcom. That is to say, a level 50 mob will be made tougher because more level 50 characters are over-equipping like level 80 characters. Over-equippers don't need to be in the majority to 'throw off the curve'. Meanwhile, the median player, the level 50 that actually fights like a level 50, gets slaughtered, as do any under-equippers.

Now, if you discourage radical over-equipping (as Gaute proposes to do by dramatically reducing it's effectiveness), then you make it easier to bring the ratio back to equilibrium. The highs aren't as high and as a result the median level characters aren't as far off from the average player toughness. In fact, if properly implemented, Funcom will have to reduce mob toughness to compensate for the lower average player toughness. Gaute's proposal commits to this.

The proposal does not call for the elimination of over-equipping; if you over-equip, you will still be able to do so, and you will still have an edge over non-over-equippers.

Does this help anyone?

I think this is what Spookymonster was trying to say about the mob toughness vs player toughness. Say you have 100 characters at level 50. Now lets say that 70 of those characters have a toughness rating of 50, and the other 30 have a toughness rating of 75. If the mob toughness is at 50, the 70 characters with a toughness of 50 will have a pretty fair fight, the other 30 will outright slaughter the mobs. So Funcom tries to keep these 30 characters from outright dominating the game by making the mob toughness an average of ALL the players toughness, which makes the mob toughness higher than the majority of the players, around 60-65.

Right now the way things work, the most efficient way to increase your damage potential is to overequip a new weapon x ql higher than your current one. In extreme cases this results in someone wielding a weapon VERY much higher than their current skill in that weapon. The majority of their increased damage potential came about through the use of temporary buffs and depending on the case, temporary implants.

What the proposed penalty to extreme overequipping would accomplish is to shift the way damage is done, and make it rely more on your ACTUAL SKILL in using that weapon than the ability to simply equip the biggest gun or sword. Sure, the person with the bigger gun will still have an advantage, but actual skill in using those weapons is MUCH more involved in the fight. Remember, skill factors into how much damage you do, but with extreme overequipping, this is almost negligible.

Penalizing extreme overequipping WILL help balance things more than you may think. Look at dual wielding for example. The damage on these weapons is generally lower than you will find on single wield weapons because they are built around the idea that you would wield two weapons somewhat close in QL. On average, most dual wield weapons that i've seen usually do 1/3 the damage of a single wield weapon. So it is difficult to compete for the person who wants to dual wield because they can only really overequip ONE weapon, and the other is limited to whatever their dual wield skill is, which at the moment cannot be buffed except through implants. So for example, things would be changed from the person with a single weapon of ql80 vs a person dual wielding a ql80/ql40, to a person with a single weapon of ql60, and a person dual wielding a ql60/ql40.

Another thing the proposed overequipping change COULD help is in the IP department. Nevermind the fact that tradeskills aren't worth very much at the moment, who actually has the IP to spare to put into these? And if you do, you probably worry if those precious points might not have better been put into something to help you get a slightly better weapon. If you knew that no matter how much you tweaked, you could only equip a gun say 20ql higher than your current level without incurring a penalty, you can opt to use a slightly smaller gun only 10ql above your level and use those IP somewhere else to diversify your character in other directions, and only take a slight hit in the damage department. For example, one soldier could aim for equipping the biggest nova flow he could possibly squeeze into without penalty. Another soldier might aim for equipping a lower ql nova flow, but put the IPS he saved into electrical engineering and weapon smithing so he can use a low light targeting scope to help him crit more, or into his nanoskills so he can use a better damage shield than the other soldier. Little nuances like that could turn the tide of battle in favor of the 2nd soldier. Whereas right now, the guy who optimizes their character to equip the biggest gun despite what any of his other skills are, pretty much wins hands down.

As I said in my previous note:

"Over-equippers don't need to be in the majority to 'throw off the curve'. "

As the number of radical over-equippers (ROEs) increases, they force the average player toughness to rise. This leads to a discrepancy between the average player toughness (add all players toughness factors together and divide by number of players) and the median player toughness (the most common toughness factor, shared by the most players).

The problem is, ROEs will NEVER hit a critical mass that allows these 2 numbers to come close to matching. As you and other ROEs have already stated, it takes a significant investment in shopping time, money (real and/or in-game), and research (exploring every new patch for loopholes, etc.). The casual gamer, comprising the majority of AO's player base, isn't interested in this. They aren't interested in making AO a full-time job; they just want to play the game and have fun.

If ROEs were an extreme minority, this wouldn't be a concern. If ROEs were in the majority, likewise, this wouldn't be a concern. But as long as the number of ROEs floats between the two, they are a problem.

Again, the problem here is not over-equipping - it is radical over-equipping.
